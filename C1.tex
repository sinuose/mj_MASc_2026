\section{Classical Description of Einstein's Brownian Motion}

The physical phenomenon of diffusion can be rigorously described as the emergent behavior atomic particle undergoing random collisions with members of the surrounding environment. In the microscopic limit, where the radius of a particle is sufficiently small and thermal fluctuations dominate its dynamics, this erratic motion is referred to as Brownian motion, or more colloquially, a diffusive process \cite{brownXXVIIBriefAccount1828}. 

The classical description of Brownian motion is concerned with how the molecular-kinetic theory of heat relates with microscopic particle suspensions in liquid or gas. In particular, how do these particles perform movements such that they can be viewed by a microscope? Einstein's argument is one of macroscopic motion. Mathematically, one can use a Taylor series expansion over a general concentration function to show a natural property of the system. Using Einstein's original notation, suppose that $\phi(\Delta)$ denotes the probability to find a particle in some space $\Delta$ subject to the normalization

\begin{equation}
	\int \phi(\Delta) d\Delta = 1
\end{equation}

Assume that the medium is isotropic such that $\phi$ is even, $\phi(\Delta) = \phi(-\Delta)$. 

Let $f(x,t)$ denote some number of particles at the point $x$. Einstein takes a finite slice of this volume and equates it to a probability space;

\begin{equation}
	f(x, t + \tau)  = \int f(x + \Delta, t)\phi(\Delta) d\Delta
\end{equation}

The argument here is that we can taylor expand both sides of this equation,

\begin{equation}
	f(x, t + \tau) \approx f(x,t) + \tau\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t}
\end{equation}
And in space;
\begin{equation}
	f(x + \Delta, t) \approx f(x,t) + \Delta \frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial x}  + \frac{\Delta^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f(x,t)}{\partial x^2} 
\end{equation}
Cancelling terms, one finds the classical diffusion equation to nearest order;
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t} = \underbrace{\left[\frac{1}{\tau}\int \frac{\Delta^2}{2} \phi(\Delta) d\Delta\right]}_{D} \frac{\partial^2 f(x,t)}{\partial x^2}
\end{equation}
Where we typically associate the diffusion coefficient with the second moment of the probability distribution
\begin{equation}
	D = \frac{1}{\tau}\int \frac{\Delta^2}{2} \phi(\Delta) d\Delta
\end{equation}
The friction coefficient here is related to the solution of the Navier-Stokes equations at a low Reynolds number. Specifically, when there are no inertial forces and $\Re \ll 1$, then the drag force on a particle,
\begin{equation}
    \vec F_d = 6\pi \eta a v,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_0 = 6 \pi \eta a$, $\eta$ is the fluid viscosity, and $a$ is the spherical particle's radius. This relation can be directly applied to the form of the diffusion coefficient to recover Einstein's famous formula;
 
\begin{equation}
	D = \frac{k_bT}{6 \pi \eta a}.
\end{equation}

\section{Langevin Dynamics}

In typical experiments, such as that of Robert Brown's pollen molecules, the observed Brownian particle moves around in a fluid volume. If one assumes that this particle exists in the thermodynamic equilibrium, it should obey the equipartion theorem. However, the observed motion appears to have more energy than that of the equipartion at times. This implies something is missing.

This missing component of motion can be described by the bombardment of the surrounding particles around it. In particular, the system experiences a \textit{random} force on it at every time step. 
\begin{equation}
	m\ddot{\vec{x}} = -\frac{\dot{\vec{x}}}{B} + \vec F(t)
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the mobility (drag term is $-\frac{\dot{\vec{x}}}{B}$), which is represented in a viscous friction term, and $\vec F(t)$ is a stochastic force term, meaning it takes a random value from some distribution $f$ for every time step $t$. This model, although easily written, is quite profound and non-trivial to calculate. Here, $x(t)$ is a stochastic process, which has a unique realization for each realization of $F(t)$.

The statistical content of $\vec F(t)$ is fixed by the moments and auto-correlation. Notably, this relation is physically associated with the fluctuation-dissipation theory which relates the drag force to the random force. In Brownian motion, this takes the form
\begin{equation}
	\langle \vec F(t)\rangle = 0, \quad \langle \vec{F}_i(t) \vec{F}_j(t')\rangle = \Gamma\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')
\end{equation}
Which stands in as delta-correlated noise, or that of a Wiener process. Notably, $\Gamma$ is constrained by the equipartion theorem. This can be shown by calculating the velocity autocorrelation function (VACF) and choosing the large $t$ limit to agree with the equipartion theorem in 3-dimensions. This fixes the fluctuation constant to being
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma = \frac{2k_bT}{B}.
\end{equation}

One can take the ensemble average of this device, and notably, extract correlation information. Rewriting in terms of velocity, and assuming zero drift velocity one finds
\begin{equation}
	m\dot{\vec{v}} = -\frac{\vec{v}}{B} + \vec A(t), \quad \int_{-\infty}^\infty A(t) dt = 0.
\end{equation}
This represents a linear differential equation with a solution hat defines the velocity autocorrelation function. This solution is 
\begin{equation}
	\langle \vec v(0)\cdot \vec v(t)\rangle = \frac{3k_bT}{m}e^{-t/(mB)}.
\end{equation}

Furthermore, when one averages the product of the above equation with $\vec x$ and integrates over time, we find the following form of the mean-square displacement. Note that $\langle v^2\rangle = \frac{3k_b T}{m}$, then the solutions of the position mean-squared displacement are written as
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
			\langle x^2\rangle &= 6k_b T m B^2\left[\frac{t}{mB} - (1-e^{-t/(mB)})\right] \\
			&\begin{cases} 
				\xrightarrow{t \ll mB} \frac{3k_b Tt^2}{m}  &  = \langle v^2\rangle t^2 \\
				\xrightarrow{t \gg mB} \frac{6k_b TmB}{m}t  &  = (6Bk_b T)t\\
			\end{cases}.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Letting $D = Bk_b T$, we recover Einstein's relation with ease. 

The unique advantage of the Langevin Formulation is that it is readily simulate-able \cite{millanNumericalSimulationsConfined2023}. It can be shown that using an Euler-Maruyama scheme, the stochastic differential equation can be integrated over realizations of $F(t)$ using a random number generator, recovering the dynamics of an ideal brownian system \cite{pathriaStatisticalMechanics2011}.

\section{From Master Equation to Fokker-Planck}

To show that the two methods are equal, we can use the typical approach to equilibrium provided by the master equation.  Suppose that $f(x,t)dx$ representation that he probability of an arbitrary particle in the ensemble has a displacement between $x$ and $x + dx$. We find that if $f$ is a probability distribution, we can write down the master equation, let $W$ represent the traditional probability transition rate;
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left[ f(x', t)W(x'|x) - f(x, t)W(x|x')\right] dx'
\end{equation}

This structure is a statement about the probability of positional transitions, which is fundamentally equivalent to the Langevin equation\footnote{Given a Markovian system.} The right hand side can be expanded using the Kramer-Moyal expansion and truncated at second order, yielding the Fokker-Planck Equation.,
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t} = - \frac{\partial }{\partial x}\left[\sigma(x) f(x,t)\right] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial x^2}\left[D(x) f(x,t)\right],
\end{equation}

where the diffusive and drift terms are parameterized over the transition probabilities,
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\sigma(x) &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \xi W(x| \xi)d\xi\\
		D(x) & = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \xi^2 W(x| \xi)d\xi.\\
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In the case of zero drift, we can clearly see that this form equates to the diffusion equation of the Langevin formulation and the Einstein-ian formulation are the same. This important property allows us to investigate solutions and dynamics of the two systems interchangeably. \section{Electrostatic Potentials}

A particle diffusing near a charged wall in an electrolyte will feel a screened electrostatic interaction. The Debye-Huckel theory is a description of the electromagnetic interactions between ions in a solution of ionic compounds. The potential in a field of ions must be solved in terms of electrostatics in a media. This is described by Poisson's equation
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) = - \frac{\rho(\vec{x})}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}
\end{equation}
In a sea of ions, we must carefully define the charge density as it pertains to the system. In a general solution of ions, there will be $j$ species of ions.
\begin{align*}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) &=- 4 \pi \frac{\rho_j(\vec{x})}{\varepsilon}
\end{align*}
The charge distribution of a single ion concentration is essentially the density of the ionic positions. It would be tedious and unfeasible to count every ion's position exactly as we are assuming too many things. Rather, one can use a statistical ensemble of ions via a canonical ensemble, which is a good approximation here.

\begin{equation}
	\rho_i(\vec{x}) = \sum_i C_i e_0 Z_i \exp{\left[-\beta Z_i e_0\Psi(\vec{x})\right]}
\end{equation}

Here the energy of the system is defined as the potential energy scaled by the charge defined as $U = qV$. The full equation is known as the Poisson-Boltzmann equation, where $\Psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the potential for all space.
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}\sum_{i =1}^\infty C_i e_0 Z_i \exp{\left[-\beta Z_i e_0\Psi(\vec{x})\right]}
\end{equation}

There is a nice approximation we can make given that the potential of the solution is small. We linearize the exponential to first order here.

\begin{align*}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) &\approx -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}\sum_{i =1}^\infty C_i e_0 Z_i \left[1-\beta Z_i e_0\Psi(\vec{x})\right] \\
	&= -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}\sum_{i =1}^\infty \left[C_i e_0 Z_i-\beta C_i Z_i^2 e_0^2\Psi(\vec{x})\right]\\
	&= -\sum_{i =1}^\infty \left[\frac{C_i e_0 Z_i}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}-\kappa_i^2 \Psi(\vec{x})\right]
\end{align*}
Where $\kappa_i^2$ is related to the debye-length, a unit of the electrostatic screening caused by the ionic interactions. The Debye length is defined as $\lambda_D = \kappa^{-1}$.

When the bulk solution is electro-neutral, $\sum_i C_i Z_i = 0$, then the zeroth therm can cleanly drop out, leaving only the approximation;
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) = \kappa^2 \Psi(\vec{x}), \quad \kappa_i^2 = \frac{\beta e_0^2}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r} \sum_i C_i Z_i^2.
\end{equation}
This equation has an obvious solution which are the real exponential functions;
\begin{equation}
	\Psi(\vec{x}) = A e^{-\kappa\vec{x}} + B e^{\kappa\vec{x}}.
\end{equation}

The pre-factor $A,B$ is the strength of the electric potential. In the case of ionic potentials, the strength of the pre-factor is a number of the number of ions and their charge density surrounding the surface. Thus, one can parameterize this pre-factor based on the geometry. 

In the approximations done in this thesis, we assume a linear symmetric potential, as well as a spherical particle between them. The form of this potential is given by 
\begin{equation}
    B = C \Gamma_p \Gamma_w,
\end{equation}
where  $|\psi|$ is a fixed amplitude due to charge density, and $\Gamma_w, \Gamma_p$ are terms associated with the Stern layers of the wall and the particle;
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        C &= \frac{64 \pi \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r a k_b T}{e_0^2}\\
        \Gamma_w &= \tanh\left(\frac{e \psi_w}{4k_b T}\right)\\
        \Gamma_p &= \tanh\left(\frac{e \zeta_p}{4k_b T}\right)\\
        \psi_w & = \frac{2 k_b T}{e} \text{asinh}\frac{e\lambda_D \sigma_w}{2 \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r k_b T}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Note that $\psi_w$ is the Stern layer of the wall related to the surface charge density of the wall \cite{israelachviliIntermolecularSurfaceForces}. This is chosen for a more intuitive measurable of a wall-like structure. 


\section{Equilibrium Distributions for Confined Systems}

In the case of interfaces, we have boundary conditions that case the particle to reflect off of the sides. Notably, the probability flux is set to 0. This implies that $\partial_x j = 0$ as well. Then, we need to solve the adjusted Fokker-Plank equation;
\begin{equation}
	j = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[D(x)f(x)\right] - \nabla V(x) f(x) 
\end{equation}

Setting $j$ to 0, we find that the solution of this equation defines the equilibrium distribution after a long time. 
\begin{equation}
	f(x) = \frac{\exp \left[-\beta\int_0^x (\frac{\nabla V(y)}{D(y)}) dy \right]}{\exp \left[-\beta \int_{-H}^{H}(\frac{\nabla V(y)}{D(y)}) dy \right]}
\end{equation}

This equation defines the equilibrium distribution of the brownian diffusion after a long time.

The particle's transverse position can be directly rendered by utilizing the scattering pattern's angular symmetry. Similarly, utilizing the phase difference in the scattering pattern between series of images the relative axial distance can be measured. Thus, the 3D positional information is encoded in the scattering pattern in iSCAT. Thus, this technique is paramount to being able to measure the diffusivity in confinement, which is why we should talk about it.

On the quantum level, electromagnetic wave field consist of photons \cite{weinberg_quantum_1995}. \begin{equation}
    I = |\vec E(\vec x, t)|^2,

\section{Scattering Microscopy}

As shown, the description of the scattered light from a sphere is full described by Eqn.\ref{eqn:lorenzmie}, known as the Lorenz-Mie scattering theory \cite{bohren1983absorption}. In the limit of particles on the order of the size of the wavelength of light, the Rayleigh model is an easier, equivalent method derived from Mie theory. 

To collect the light, there have been three main schemes used in literature; forward, side, and back-scattering microscopy schemes.

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:ipsf_sim}, the scattering distribution taken at different projections widely varies. In addition, the amount of light that gets collected in each of these length-scales also varies.

In the back-scattering scheme, the light collected is the sum of the incident light and the scattered light.

\begin{equation}
    I(x,y) = |\vec E_r + \vec E_s|^2 
\end{equation}

given that the incident wave is known as a polarized plane wave, this equation can be rewritten for the scattered light;
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I = E_rE_r^* + E_sE_s^* + 2\Re{\left[E_rE_s\right]} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The incident field will be modulated by a scattering term, which is the Rayleigh scattering amplitude, $S$, as well as the reference term, $R$. Thus, the total intensity will be the sum of the scattering amplitude, reference amplitude, and the phase interference between the two fields; 
\begin{equation}
    I = |E_i|^2\left[|R|^2 + |S|^2 + 2|R||S|\cos \Delta \varphi\right], \quad \Delta \varphi = \varphi_s \varphi_r
\end{equation}


It should now be noted that the angle of image formation has not been specified. This is of importance as it allows to discuss the various methods of scattering microscopy without loss of generality between methods. Notably, the main difference is the angular content which is obtained from the various scattering models. In the case of spheres, this means using the various angular slices of the resultant scattered field. 

\section{Interferometric Light Scattering (iSCAT)}

The key assumption in iSCAT is the relative magnitude of terms. The reference field is constant. The scattering term $R \approx k^4\alpha^2 = \frac{V^2}{\lambda^4}$. Similarly, the inference term scales as the square root of this. Thus, the scattering term is dominated by the two fields, which we can ignore. 

To accurately measure the positions, we can define the contrast metric, which relates the total field to that of a reference field;
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        C &= \frac{I - |E_r|^2}{|E_r|^2} \\
        &= \frac{|S|^2 + 2|R||S|\cos \Delta \varphi}{|R|^2} \\
        &\xrightarrow{|S| \ll |R|} \frac{2|S|}{|R|}\cos\Delta \varphi
    \end{aligned} 
\end{equation}

The phase of the interference consists of a sum of terms consisting of phases accumulated through the traversal of the scattered field. The total phase is dominated by the Gouy phase, which consists of phase accumulated when light is passed through a converging and diverging field, the defocus phase, which can be parameterized to a relative phase shift, as well as the phase accumulated phase difference, which is typical of a Michaelson-Interferomer type system. \section{Resolution limits of optical microscopy}

The last thing to touch upon is understanding how the image that is being enlarged arrives to the camera, which consists of discussion of optical aberrations. 

As mentioned in the formulation for the electric field scatter we have that any optical field consists of a set of plane waves that construct and deconstruct to form a field. That is, the electric field lies in the basis of a fourier series of terms of complex exponential functions. This fact allows one to describe the propagation of a wave in throughout z.
\begin{equation}
	\vec E(x,y,z) = \iint_{\Omega} \vec E_0(k_x, k_y) e^{-i\left(x k_x + y k_y + \sqrt{k^2 - k_x^2 - k_y^2} \right)}dk_x dk_y, \quad \Omega = \{x \in \R : x^2 \leq k^2\} 
\end{equation}
We have that the energy of the wave is fixed by the trigonometric relation of the transverse wave vectors. Notably, this is fixed by the relation $|k| = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$.

The propagated wave passes through an objective which can be approximated by a lens. Using the ABCD matrix formulation, we can find that to have a clear image, the imaging condition must be satisfied, that is
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{z_1} + \frac{1}{z_2} = \frac{1}{f}
\end{equation}
where $z_1, z_2$ are the positions of the observer and the object, and $f$ is the focal distance of the lens. Thus, the magnification of the lens is defined as $M = z_2/z_1$. However, to understand the aberrations it is necessary to have a field formulation of this problem. The simplest way to do this is described using linear optics theory.
 
Briefly, we define a pupil function $P(x,y)$ which is unitary if $r\leq R$, where $R$ is the radius of lens. The point spread function of the object is then defined as a delta function through a two lens system. This is given by the fourier transform of the point source masked by the pupil function;
\begin{eqnarray}
	H(x,y) = \iint_\Omega P(x',y')e^{\frac{ik}{2}\left(\frac{1}{z_1} - \frac{1}{f_1} - \frac{1}{f_2} + \frac{1}{z_2}\right)\sqrt{{x'}^2} + {y'}^2}e^{-\frac{ik}{z_2}(xx' + yy')}dx'dy'
\end{eqnarray}
Expanding this, one can use a zero-order Hankel transform to integrate over the radius and find that the point-spread function, $H$, is defined in terms of the NA and the magnification;
\begin{equation}
	H(r, z) = \int_0^1e^{-\frac{ik}{2}\frac{\text{NA}^2}{n}\rho^2 z}J_0\left(\frac{k\text{NA}}{M}\rho r\right) \rho d\rho
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the rescaled radial vector $\rho= r'/R$, $\text{NA} = nR/f_1$, and $M = nf_2/f_1$.

In perfect focus, we have that $z=0$, for $z\neq0$ we have a defocusing aberration which is defined as the secondary exponential. For a point source, which is diffraction limited, we find that the following is the limit of the optical microscope;
\begin{equation}
	H(r,0) = |\frac{J_1(k\text{NA}r)}{k\text{NA} r}|^2 
\end{equation}
We can solve that the minimum distance between the peak of the PSF and the minimum is solved by $k\text{NA}r = x_0$ where $x_0$ is the first root of $J_1(x)$. This is classical known as the minimum transverse imaging criterion;
\begin{equation}
	r_{\text{min}} = 0.61 \frac{\lambda}{\text{NA}}
\end{equation}
Similar results are found for the minimum axial imaging criterion by setting $r=0$ and integrating. 
\begin{equation}
	z_{\text{min}} = \frac{2\lambda n}{\text{NA}^2}
\end{equation}

 At this point, we invite the fact that not all the light that scatters passes through the lens. Specifically, the range of $k_x$ and $k_y$ are fundamentally limited by the wavelength. The resolution limit is $\lambda/\text{NA}$. In the case of scattering microscopy, this is not a large problem as the scattering pattern is much larger, however, in the limit of two particle near each other, the spatial resolution limit must be enforced. 

 \section{Sources of noise in imaging}

In imaging, there is a number of sources of aberrations that one must account for. To discuss these sources of noise, a list of noises will be formed as one goes through the process of taking an image.

The schematic of an iSCAT microscope was already shown in Fig. \ref{fig:schematic}, however, one can use a simplified model system without loss of generality. For a general imaging system, one only requires a single lens for imaging. This simple setup is shown in Fig \ref{fig:imaging_diagram_simple}.

The lens will collects light from the object plane, $U_o$ to the imaging plane, $U_i$, or the camera's plane. The propagation of that wave will collect aberration from the len's point spread function, as well as phase accumulation. The traversal over the point spread function is 
\begin{equation}
    U_i(x_i, y_i) = \iint_{\R^2} h(x_i, y_i, x_o, y_o) U_o(x_o, y_o) dx_o dy_o,
\end{equation} 
where $h(x_i, y_i, x_o, y_o)$ is known as the optical transfer function, or the response of a lens to a point-source, modelled as a delta-function. The len's magnification is incorporated into this using the following approximation;
\begin{equation}
    h(x_i, y_i, x_o, y_o) \approx K \delta(x_i \pm M x_o, y_i \pm M y_o)
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a complex constant and $M$ is a real-valued magnification. We can see that because of this, our image is spatially limited by this point-spread, which was earlier shown by the diffraction limit. 

In addition to the spatial noise, there is a phase accumulation known as the Guoy phase. As the light, which can be assumed as consisting of an Eigen-expansion of plane waves, passes through a lens, these plane waves will converge to a gaussian beam with a beam-waist around the focal point. As this focusing occurs, there is a phase that is accumulated known as the Guoy phase. This phase accumulation is equal to
\begin{equation}
    \psi(z) = \arctan\left(\frac{z}{z_R}\right), \quad z_r = \frac{\pi w_0^2 n}{\lambda},
\end{equation}
where $w_0$ is the waist of the final beam, $n$ is the refractive index, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light. In imaging, as long as the refractive index is constant, there is a total phase shift of $\pi$. 

In the presence of a background beam, there will be a presence of a background. This background's source is the illumination beam itself, in the case of scattering microscopy. This background noise profile will take the shape of the illumination profile alongside the intensity of the power density. 

The camera used will also contain electronic noise associated with it. For CMOS cameras, the noise present will consist of photon noise from the detectors and readout noise from the data-transfer. These noises can be modelled as Poisson noise from the photon detections and a gaussian for the readout noise. 

In the case of using an imperfect lens, or a set of lenses as one has with an objective as one does in high magnification microscopy, there is a risk of spherical aberration. Spherical aberrations are caused by imperfections and misalignments in the lens itself. This cases the focal point of the lens to be slightly different depending on which area the light is focused by lens. For imaging purposes, spherical aberrations cause various parts of the image to be out of focus, while others are in focus, creating non-uniform images.

\chapter{Biological Application}

As we have described, the diffusivity of a spherical particle is directly linked to a motion that is thermally driven. It happens that in the nature of biological cells, this diffusive motion is a driver for many reactions, such as enzyme-protein coupling, protein-protein coupling, and vesicle binding. Thus, it is of utmost importance to adequately understand the confinement effect of diffusion in a given system. 

Application-wise, the confinement of nanoparticles is directly related to fabrication and development research. Notably, since nanoparticle fabrication involves methods of quality control, these methods must be able to quantify between different measures of noise impending their measurement. In the case of confinement effects, these effects often hinder diffusion, as described, which can indirectly bias the final reported measurement. 

\section{Diffusion in cells}

The cellular system consists of many different components co-existing in the cellular cytoplasm. To get a survey of sizes, the organelles are on the order of $1-\SI{10}{\micro \meter}$. while smaller proteins and enzymes, such as ribosomes, vesicles, and lysosomes are on the order of $10-\SI{100}{\nano \meter}$. 

This beings said, the motion is directly linked to the fluidic properties of the cytoplasm. In the case of a eukaryotic cell, the Reynold's number is low, which implies an overdamped langevin equation. In addition, the thermal scale is that of high temperature, or $\sim \SI{310}{\kelvin}$. In addition, the comparison between diffusion and advection is important. For diffusion to dominate we need that the diffusive timescale is must shorter. Here, we find that since the Peclet number is proportional to the characteristics length $L$, while the reynolds number is $L^2$. for small characteristic lengths, such as those proposed, we see that the diffusive term is clearly shorter. 

\section{Brownian motion as a method for measurements of size in biology}

As previously shown, we can infer the stoke's einstein diffusion relation to directly measure the size of a biological diffusing particle. In particular, this relation can be explicitly formalized for the hydrodynamic radius of a spherical nanoparticle.
\begin{equation}
    a_h = \frac{k_b T}{6\pi \eta D}
\end{equation}
Methods such as nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA) or single-molecule particle tracking (SPT) are based on this method. 

However, there are other methods that can be used. As this thesis is not concerned with fluorescent methods, they will not be explained in depth, however, fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) works on similar ideas of measuring diffusion autocorrelation curves using a fluorescent probe. 

In compliment to single molecule, there are bulk methods as well. In particular, dynamic light scattering (DLS) allows to measure the size of molecules based on their shape function. In particular, the scattering intensity can be weighting to the inverse of $a^6$, which in turn returns the size of the sample. However, these methods are ill-posed for poly-disperse sample, which is one of the weaknesses of the DLS method. 

\section{Lipid Nanoparticles}

The biological molecule of choice for this investigation of confinement is a useful one with lots of applications - the lipid nanoparticle (LNP).

First, it is necessary to identify with the liposome. The liposome, as a macroscopic object, is a spherical membrane of lipid subunit, each carrying a charge and hydrophobicity. The size of a single submit is on the level of angstroms, however, the final liposome can be between $\SI{1}{\nano \meter}-\SI{10}{\micro \meter}$. Liposomes and cellular vesicles are very similar, which naturally posed the question of drug delivery using a liposome model. 

In recent years, the LNP was formulated - a collection of lipid based walls that are used to encapsulate a drug or cargo. These have applications in drug delivery and have been using in the Sars-Covid vaccine in 2020. These molecules are very useful and thus it is important to know how to improve them. 

Modern tools to image and characterize the LNP are split into single-molecule techniques and bulk assays. In the realm of single-molecule methods, CLiC has been shown to be a high-throughput, accurate, and reliable method to characterize these particle's properties.
