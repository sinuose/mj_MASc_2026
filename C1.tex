\chapter{Brownian Motion}
\label{chap:brownianmotion}


The physical phenomenon of irregular movements of nanoparticles in liquid is known as Brownian motion. The observation of this motion originated around the year 1827 when Robert Brown observed a stochastic motion of clay particles originating from plant pollen under his microscope \cite{brownXXVIIBriefAccount1828}. This stochastic motion is known as \textit{Brownian Motion} and is invertibly related to the size of the particle.

% For relatively large particles, the optical microscope is a good tool to observe them. However, for particles smaller the nano-scale, the diffraction limit is a property that must be overcome. This was theoretically described by Abbe \cite{Steck2021,Born1999}.

This chapter aims to establish the theoretical background underlying the relationships between the diffusivity, particle size, and spatial coordinate. This process requires sufficient mathematical detail as no single displacement carries enough information to define the whole trace, however, as one observes this motion for a longer period of time, the randomness tends to converge to a lawful, physical origin. 

This chapter introduces the mathematics of Brownian motion and diffusion-type processes. Einstein's diffusion- equation argument and Langevin's stochastic equation of motion are discussed and shown to be equivalent through the Fokker-Planck formalism. In the case of diffusion under confinement, where hydrodynamic wall effects modify the mobility and electrostatic interactions, described by the Debye-Hückel interactions, govern particle-wall interactions in ionic solution. Finally, the subject of equilibrium distributions  are discussed with regards to  spatial probability distributions arising in confined diffusion processes. 


\section{Einstein's Brownian Motion}

The physical phenomenon of diffusion can be rigorously described as the emergent behavior atomic particle undergoing random collisions with members of the surrounding environment. In the microscopic limit, where the radius of a particle is sufficiently small and thermal fluctuations dominate its dynamics, this erratic motion is referred to as Brownian motion, or more colloquially, a diffusive process \cite{brownXXVIIBriefAccount1828}. 

The classical description of Brownian motion is concerned with how the molecular-kinetic theory of heat relates with microscopic particle suspensions in liquid or gas. In particular, how do these particles perform movements such that they can be viewed by a microscope? Einstein's argument is one of macroscopic motion. Mathematically, one can use a Taylor series expansion over a general concentration function to show a natural property of the system. Using Einstein's original notation, suppose that $\phi(\Delta)$ denotes the probability to find a particle in some space $\Delta$ subject to the normalization

\begin{equation}
	\int \phi(\Delta) d\Delta = 1
\end{equation}

Assume that the medium is isotropic such that $\phi$ is even, $\phi(\Delta) = \phi(-\Delta)$. 

Let $f(x,t)$ denote some number of particles at the point $x$. Einstein takes a finite slice of this volume and equates it to a probability space;

\begin{equation}
	f(x, t + \tau)  = \int f(x + \Delta, t)\phi(\Delta) d\Delta
\end{equation}

The argument here demonstrated by a Taylor-expansion of both sides of this equation,

\begin{equation}
	f(x, t + \tau) \approx f(x,t) + \tau\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t}.
\end{equation}
In the spatial dimensions,
\begin{equation}
	f(x + \Delta, t) \approx f(x,t) + \Delta \frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial x}  + \frac{\Delta^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f(x,t)}{\partial x^2}.
\end{equation}
Cancelling terms, the classical diffusion equation is shown to nearest order;
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t} = \underbrace{\left[\frac{1}{\tau}\int \frac{\Delta^2}{2} \phi(\Delta) d\Delta\right]}_{D} \frac{\partial^2 f(x,t)}{\partial x^2},
\end{equation}
where the diffusion coefficient is associated with the second moment of the probability distribution
\begin{equation}
	D = \frac{1}{\tau}\int \frac{\Delta^2}{2} \phi(\Delta) d\Delta.
\end{equation}
The friction coefficient here is related to the solution of the Navier-Stokes equations at a low Reynolds number. Specifically, when there are no inertial forces and $\Re \ll 1$, then the drag force on a particle,
\begin{equation}
    \vec F_d = 6\pi \eta a v,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_0 = 6 \pi \eta a$, $\eta$ is the fluid viscosity, and $a$ is the spherical particle's radius. This relation can be directly applied to the form of the diffusion coefficient to recover Einstein's famous formula;
 
\begin{equation}
	D = \frac{k_bT}{6 \pi \eta a}.
\end{equation}

\section{Langevin Dynamics}

In single-particle movement-based experiments, such as observations of Robert Brown's particulates or tracking of diffusing lipid-nanoparticles, the observed Brownian particle move within a fluid volume. If one assumes that this particle exists in the thermodynamic equilibrium, it should obey the equipartion theorem. However, the observed motion appears to have more energy than predicted by the equipartion at certain times, indicating that an additional contribution to the dynamics must be considered \cite{Caspi2000EnhancedDiffusion, Mizuno2007NonequilibriumMechanics}.

This missing component of motion can be described by the bombardment of the surrounding particles in the liquid. In particular, the system experiences a \textit{random} force on it at every time step. 
\begin{equation}
	m\ddot{\vec{x}} = -\frac{\dot{\vec{x}}}{B} + \vec F(t)
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the mobility (drag term is $-\frac{\dot{\vec{x}}}{B}$), which is represented in a viscous friction term, and $\vec F(t)$ is a stochastic force term, meaning it takes a random value from some distribution $f$ for every time step $t$. This model, although easily written, is quite profound and its analytical treatment is non-trivial. Here, $x(t)$ is a stochastic process, where each realization of $F(t)$ produces a unique trajectory of $x(t)$.

The statistical content of $\vec F(t)$ is determined by the moments and auto-correlation. Notably, this relation is physically described by the fluctuation-dissipation theory, which relates the drag force to the random force. In Brownian motion, this takes the form
\begin{equation}
	\langle \vec F(t)\rangle = 0, \quad \langle \vec{F}_i(t) \vec{F}_j(t')\rangle = \Gamma\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')
\end{equation}
Which stands in as delta-correlated noise, or that of a Wiener process. Notably, $\Gamma$ is constrained by the equipartion theorem. This can be shown by calculating the velocity autocorrelation function (VACF) and choosing the large $t$ limit to agree with the equipartion theorem in 3-dimensions. This fixes the fluctuation constant to being
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma = \frac{2k_bT}{B}.
\end{equation}

One can take the ensemble average of this device, and notably, extract correlation information. Rewriting in terms of velocity, and assuming zero drift velocity one finds
\begin{equation}
	m\dot{\vec{v}} = -\frac{\vec{v}}{B} + \vec A(t), \quad \int_{-\infty}^\infty A(t) dt = 0.
\end{equation}
This represents a linear differential equation with a solution hat defines the velocity autocorrelation function. This solution is 
\begin{equation}
	\langle \vec v(0)\cdot \vec v(t)\rangle = \frac{3k_bT}{m}e^{-t/(mB)}.
\end{equation}

Furthermore, when one averages the product of the above equation with $\vec x$ and integrates over time, the form of the mean-square displacement is found. Note that $\langle v^2\rangle = \frac{3k_b T}{m}$, then the solutions of the position mean-squared displacement are written as
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
			\langle x^2\rangle &= 6k_b T m B^2\left[\frac{t}{mB} - (1-e^{-t/(mB)})\right] \\
			&\begin{cases} 
				\xrightarrow{t \ll mB} \frac{3k_b Tt^2}{m}  &  = \langle v^2\rangle t^2 \\
				\xrightarrow{t \gg mB} \frac{6k_b TmB}{m}t  &  = (6Bk_b T)t\\
			\end{cases}.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Letting $D = Bk_b T$, Einstein's relation is recovered. 

The unique advantage of the Langevin Formulation is that it is readily simulate-able \cite{millanNumericalSimulationsConfined2023}. It can be shown that using an Euler-Maruyama scheme, the stochastic differential equation can be integrated over realizations of $F(t)$ using a random number generator, recovering the dynamics of an ideal brownian system \cite{pathriaStatisticalMechanics2011}.

\section{From Master Equation to Fokker-Planck}

To show that the two methods are equal, the master equation can be shown to validate equilibrium distributions.  Suppose that $f(x,t)dx$ representation that he probability of an arbitrary particle in the ensemble has a displacement between $x$ and $x + dx$. If $f$ is a probability distribution,  the master equation can be represented. Let $W$ represent the traditional probability transition rate, then
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left[ f(x', t)W(x'|x) - f(x, t)W(x|x')\right] dx'.
\end{equation}

This structure is a statement about the probability of positional transitions, which is fundamentally equivalent to the Langevin equation\footnote{Given a Markovian system.} The right hand side can be expanded using the Kramer-Moyal expansion and truncated at second order, yielding the Fokker-Planck Equation.,
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial f(x,t)}{\partial t} = - \frac{\partial }{\partial x}\left[\sigma(x) f(x,t)\right] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial x^2}\left[D(x) f(x,t)\right],
\end{equation}

where the diffusive and drift terms are parameterized over the transition probabilities,
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\sigma(x) &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \xi W(x| \xi)d\xi\\
		D(x) & = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \xi^2 W(x| \xi)d\xi.\\
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In the case of zero drift, the form of this equation equates to the diffusion equation of the Langevin formulation. Thus,  the Einsteinian and Langevin formulation are the same. This important property allows us to investigate solutions and dynamics of the two systems interchangeably. In particular, relating expected results from simulation-based behavior of Langevin problems.

\section{Confined Diffusion Processes}

The effect of diffusion near an interface on a nearby particle is quite particular. The effect on confinement can be first imagined as the effects from the fluid friction flow. Notably, the particle being immersed in fluid invites a stokes friction which effects the perpendicular and parallel flows. Hiding Faxén derived the relations in no-slip boundary conditions near a brownian particle \cite{Faxen1921,bian111YearsBrownian2016}; 
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\mu_\parallel\left(\frac{a}{h}\right) &\approx \mu_\infty \left[1 - \frac{9}{16} \left(\frac{a}{h}\right)  + \frac{1}{8} \left(\frac{a}{h}\right)^3 - \frac{45}{256} \left(\frac{a}{h}\right)^4 - \frac{1}{16} \left(\frac{a}{h}\right)^5\right]\\
		\mu_\perp \left(\frac{a}{h}\right) &\approx \mu_\infty \left[1 - \frac{9}{8} \left(\frac{a}{h}\right)  + \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{a}{h}\right)^3  \right]\\
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In the case of a two-wall confinement, the form of the diffusion coefficient has a lot of depth in wealth. In application, it has been shown that the form of the Onseen drag in terms of mobility coefficients.
\begin{equation}
	\gamma_2(z) \approx \gamma_0 + [\gamma_1(z) - \gamma_0] + [\gamma_1(H-z) - \gamma_0] 
\end{equation}


\section{Self-consistent Confinement coefficient}

A consequence of the confined mobility relations presented by Faxén is the diffusion correction, which relates the bulk diffusivity to the confined axial or transverse diffusion coefficients.
\begin{equation}
    D_0 = \mu_\parallel^{-1} D_\parallel = \mu_\perp^{-1} D_\perp.
\end{equation}
The values of the corrections are, however, functions of the particle radius. For the Stokes-Einstein relation and the Faxén mobility corrections to be consistent, the following relation must hold.
\begin{equation}
    \frac{k_bT}{6\pi\eta a} = \mu_\parallel^{-1}\left(a, h\right) \hat{D_\parallel} = \mu_\perp^{-1}\left(a, h\right) \hat{D_\perp},
\end{equation}
where the $\hat{\cdot}$ indicates measured values. This condition is self-consistent with a general mobility that is dependent on the particle radius if the particle height is known. One useful property of the Faxén functions, as well as the linear-superposition approximations are that they are monotonically decreasing. This is illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:mob_coef}.

\section{Electrostatic Potentials}

A particle diffusing near a charged wall in an electrolyte will feel a screened electrostatic interaction. The Debye-Huckel theory is a description of the electromagnetic interactions between ions in a solution of ionic compounds. The potential in a field of ions must be solved in terms of electrostatics in a media. This is described by Poisson's equation
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) = - \frac{\rho(\vec{x})}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}
\end{equation}
In a general solution of ions, there will be $j$ species of ions.
\begin{align*}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) &=- 4 \pi \frac{\rho_j(\vec{x})}{\varepsilon}
\end{align*}
The charge distribution of a single ion concentration is essentially the density of the ionic positions. It would be tedious and unfeasible to count every ion's position exactly. Rather, one can use a statistical ensemble of ions via a canonical ensemble, which is a good approximation here.

\begin{equation}
	\rho_i(\vec{x}) = \sum_i C_i e_0 Z_i \exp{\left[-\beta Z_i e_0\Psi(\vec{x})\right]}
\end{equation}

Here the energy of the system is defined as the potential energy scaled by the charge defined as $U = qV$. The full equation is known as the Poisson-Boltzmann equation, where $\Psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the potential for all space.
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}\sum_{i =1}^\infty C_i e_0 Z_i \exp{\left[-\beta Z_i e_0\Psi(\vec{x})\right]}
\end{equation}

There is a nice approximation that can be linearized to first order,

\begin{align*}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) &\approx -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}\sum_{i =1}^\infty C_i e_0 Z_i \left[1-\beta Z_i e_0\Psi(\vec{x})\right] \\
	&= -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}\sum_{i =1}^\infty \left[C_i e_0 Z_i-\beta C_i Z_i^2 e_0^2\Psi(\vec{x})\right]\\
	&= -\sum_{i =1}^\infty \left[\frac{C_i e_0 Z_i}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}-\kappa_i^2 \Psi(\vec{x})\right].
\end{align*}
Where $\kappa_i^2$ is related to the debye-length, a unit of the electrostatic screening caused by the ionic interactions. The Debye length is defined as $\lambda_D = \kappa^{-1}$.

When the bulk solution is electro-neutral, $\sum_i C_i Z_i = 0$, then the zeroth therm can cleanly drop out, leaving only the approximation;
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \Psi(\vec{x}) = \kappa^2 \Psi(\vec{x}), \quad \kappa_i^2 = \frac{\beta e_0^2}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r} \sum_i C_i Z_i^2.
\end{equation}
This equation has an obvious solution which are the real exponential functions;
\begin{equation}
	\Psi(\vec{x}) = A e^{-\kappa\vec{x}} + B e^{\kappa\vec{x}}.
\end{equation}

The pre-factor $A,B$ is the strength of the electric potential. In the case of ionic potentials, the strength of the pre-factor is a number of the number of ions and their charge density surrounding the surface. Thus, one can parameterize this pre-factor based on the geometry. 

In the approximations done in this thesis,  a linear symmetric potential, as well as a spherical particle between the potential is assumed. The form of this potential is given by 
\begin{equation}
    B = C \Gamma_p \Gamma_w,
\end{equation}
where  $|\psi|$ is a fixed amplitude due to charge density, and $\Gamma_w, \Gamma_p$ are terms associated with the Stern layers of the wall and the particle;
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        C &= \frac{64 \pi \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r a k_b T}{e_0^2}\\
        \Gamma_w &= \tanh\left(\frac{e \psi_w}{4k_b T}\right)\\
        \Gamma_p &= \tanh\left(\frac{e \zeta_p}{4k_b T}\right)\\
        \psi_w & = \frac{2 k_b T}{e} \text{asinh}\frac{e\lambda_D \sigma_w}{2 \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r k_b T}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Note that $\psi_w$ is the Stern layer of the wall related to the surface charge density of the wall \cite{israelachviliIntermolecularSurfaceForces}. This is chosen for a more intuitive measurable of a wall-like structure. 


\section{Equilibrium Distributions for Confined Systems}

In the case of interfaces, the boundary conditions state that the must particle reflect off of the sides. Notably, the probability flux is set to 0. This implies that $\partial_x j = 0$ as well. Thus, the adjusted Fokker-Plank equation must be solved,
\begin{equation}
	j = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[D(x)f(x)\right] - \nabla V(x) f(x).
\end{equation}

Setting $j$ to 0, the solution of this equation defines the equilibrium distribution after a long time. 
\begin{equation}
	f(x) = \frac{\exp \left[-\beta\int_0^x (\frac{\nabla V(y)}{D(y)}) dy \right]}{\exp \left[-\beta \int_{-H}^{H}(\frac{\nabla V(y)}{D(y)}) dy \right]}
\end{equation}

This equation defines the equilibrium distribution of the brownian diffusion after a long time.

\section{Brownian motion as a method for measurements of size in biology}

As previously shown, the Stokes-Einstein diffusion relation is inferred to directly measure the size of a biological diffusing particle. In particular, this relation can be explicitly formalized for the hydrodynamic radius of a spherical nanoparticle.
\begin{equation}
    a = \frac{k_b T}{6\pi \eta D(\vec{x})}
\end{equation}
Methods such as nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA) or single-molecule particle tracking (SPT) are based on this method. 

However, there are other methods that can be used. As this thesis is not concerned with fluorescent methods, they will not be explained in depth, however, fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) works on similar ideas of measuring diffusion autocorrelation curves using a fluorescent probe. 

In compliment to single molecule, there are bulk methods as well. In particular, dynamic light scattering (DLS) allows to measure the size of molecules based on their shape function. In particular, the scattering intensity can be weighting to the inverse of $a^6$, which in turn returns the size of the sample. However, these methods are ill-posed for poly-disperse sample, which is one of the weaknesses of the DLS method.

\chapter{Interferometric Scattering Principles}
\label{chap:iscatprinciples}

To determine the physical properties of nanoparticles suspended in a fluid media, tracking movement of nanoparticles and reconstructing their spatial trajectories must be performed. To achieve this, a particle localization method that maintains a consistent signal over extended measurement periods is required. One method that enables such localization of particle traces is interferometric scattering microscopy, iSCAT \cite{taylor_interferometric_2018}. 

iSCAT is a relatively modern optical imaging technique that utilizes light scattering contrast to localize particle signal. The particle's transverse position can be directly rendered by utilizing the scattering pattern's angular symmetry. Similarly, utilizing the phase difference in the scattering pattern between series of images, the relative axial distance can be measured. The 3D positional information is encoded in the scattering pattern in iSCAT.

\section{Electromagnetic Scattering of Homogenous Spheres}

When a homogenous particle is subjected to light produced by a monochromatic laser, a diffractive pattern is produced upon scattering. These patterns can be captured by a camera, however, to extract positional information from this pattern, a mathematical model is required to describe it. 

The optical theory of light will obey the Maxwell's equations, which motivates solutions to obey the wave equation for the electric and magnetic fields
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \vec E + k^2 \vec E = 0, \quad \vec H = \frac{1}{i \omega \mu_0} \nabla \times \vec E.
\end{equation}

The eigenfunctions of the wave equation are the harmonics of the Laplacian operator, $\nabla^2$, in an arbitrary coordinate basis. For isotropic scattering, radial scattering, the spherical basis has been used in the literature \cite{bohren1983absorption}. The solutions of the vector-wave equation are known as the vector spherical harmonics. 

Let $\psi$ be a solution to the scalar wave equation,
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \psi + k^2 \psi = 0,
\end{equation} then the function generator of the general solution is taken by the sum of the components. This solution is given by the separable form \cite{jackson_classical_1999},
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \psi(r, \theta, \phi) &= \Phi(\phi) \Theta(\theta) R(r), \\
        \Phi(\phi) & \in \{\cos(m\phi), \sin(m\phi)\}, \\
        \Theta(\theta) &= P^m_n(\cos\theta),\\
        R(r) &\in \{j_n, y_n, j_n + i y_n, j_n - i y_n\}. \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The symbols $j_n, y_n$ represent the spherical Bessel functions. Their complex sums, $j_n \pm i y_n$,  are the Hankel functions. The vector spherical harmonics are best described by separating these solutions into even and odd solutions as their pre-factor will be $\cos$ or $\sin$ respectively. This is found in Eqns 4.17 to 4.20 in book written by Bohren \cite{bohren1983absorption},
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \vec{M}_{emn} = \vec \nabla \times (\vec r \psi_{emn}), \quad \vec{M}_{omn} = \vec \nabla \times (\vec r \psi_{omn}), \\
        \vec{N}_{emn} = \frac{1}{k}\vec \nabla \times \vec{M}_{emn}, \quad \vec{N}_{omn} = \frac{1}{k}\vec \nabla \times \vec{M}_{omn.} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

A scattering solution will consist of incident and scattering light. The incident light is assumed to be a polarized plane wave along the $x$ axis without loss of generality. The general form of these equations are then the sum of the spherical vector harmonics. When subjected to the energy conservation boundary condition written as
\begin{equation}
    (\vec E_i + \vec E_s - \vec E_1) \times \hat r = (\vec H_i + \vec H_s - \vec H_1) \times \hat r = 0,
\end{equation}
and the orthogonality of the spherical vector harmonics is utilized, the exact solution can be found. This is known as the Mie Theory form of the scattering of light off of a dielectric sphere \cite{bohren1983absorption},

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:lorenzmie}
    \begin{aligned}
     \vec E_i &= E_0 e^{ikr\cos \theta}\hat x \\    
     \vec E_s &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty E_n(i a_n \vec{N^{(1)}}_{e1n} - b_n \vec{M^{(1)}}_{o1n}),\\
     \vec E_1 &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty E_n(i c_n \vec{M^{(1)}}_{o1n} - d_n \vec{N^{(1)}}_{e1n}).     
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In this thesis, only the scattering field was taken into consideration. The amplitude factors of interest are,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        a_n &= \frac{m\Psi(mx)\Psi'(x) - \Psi(x)\Psi'(mx)}{m\Psi(mx)\xi'(x) -\xi(x) \Psi'(mx)},\\
        b_n &= \frac{\Psi(mx)\Psi'(x) - m\Psi(x)\Psi'(mx)}{\Psi(mx)\xi'(x) - m \xi(x) \Psi'(mx)},\\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\Psi, \xi$ are the Riccatti-Bessel functions \cite{bohren1983absorption}. 

\section{Rayleigh Scattering}

As the particle size decreases and is observed from far away, approximations can be made to simplify the form of the equations. These approximations can be taken without degrading the length-scale of interest. Notably, as the product, $kr \ll 1$, the Hankel function approaches the form of a spherical wave function,
\begin{equation}
    h_n(kr) \xrightarrow{kr \gg 1} (-i)^{n+1}\frac{e^{ikr}}{kr}.
\end{equation}

In the length-scale where particles are of comparable size to the wavelength of light, $a_1 \approx \frac{-2i}{3}(ka)^3 \frac{m^2 -1 }{m^2 + 2}$, and $b_1 \approx 0$. Thus for $n=1$, $\pi_1 = 1, \tau_1 = \cos \theta$. Thus, the vector spherical harmonics are approximated by the spherical wave forms,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        M^{(3)} &\approx (-i)^{n+1}\frac{e^{ikr}}{kr} \bracket{\cos\phi \pi_n \hat \theta - \sin\phi \tau_n \hat \phi}, \\
        N^{(3)} &\approx (-i)^{n+1}\frac{e^{ikr}}{kr} \bracket{\cos\phi \pi_n \hat \theta - \sin\phi \tau_n \hat \phi}. \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Using these two equations, the scattering equation, known as the Rayleigh approximation, describes the full scattering field as a simple sum,

\begin{equation}
    \vec E_s = \frac{i E_0 e^{ikr}}{kr}\sbracket{\hat \theta \cos \phi \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2n +1}{n(n+1)}(a_n \tau_n + b_n \pi_n) + \hat \phi \sin \phi \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2n +1}{n(n+1)}(a_n \pi_n + b_n \tau_n)}.
\end{equation}

After substituting the terms for $n=1$, the sum reduces significantly to the equations for transverse and axial scattering fields,

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
            \vec E_{s, \perp} &=  \vec E_{i, \perp} \frac{k^2 \alpha e^{ikr}}{r},\\
            \vec E_{s, \parallel} &=  \vec E_{i, \parallel} \frac{k^2 \alpha e^{ikr}}{r} \cos \theta.\\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Singling out the scattering field and noting the form of the wave-vector, $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda/n}$, the scattering field of spheres on the order of the length of the wavelength of the incident light is written in the seminal form referenced in literature,
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:rayleigh_scat}
    \vec E_s = \vec E_0 e^{ikr}\frac{\sqrt{8}\alpha \pi^2 n^2_m}{\lambda^2 r} \sqrt{1 + \cos \theta}.
\end{equation}

In the limit of particles on the order of the size of the wavelength of light, the Rayleigh model is an easier, equivalent method derived from Mie theory. 

To collect the light, there have been three main schemes availible; forward, side, and back-scattering microscopy schemes.

In the back-scattering scheme, the light collected is the sum of the incident light and the scattered light,

\begin{equation}
    I(x,y) = |\vec E_r + \vec E_s|^2.
\end{equation}

Given that the incident wave is known as a polarized plane wave, this equation can be rewritten for the scattered light;
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I = E_rE_r^* + E_sE_s^* + 2\Re{\left[E_rE_s\right]}. \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The incident field will be modulated by a scattering term, which is the Rayleigh scattering amplitude, $S$, as well as the reference term, $R$. Thus, the total intensity will be the sum of the scattering amplitude, reference amplitude, and the phase interference between the two fields; 
\begin{equation}
    I = |E_i|^2\left[|R|^2 + |S|^2 + 2|R||S|\cos \Delta \varphi\right], \quad \Delta \varphi = \varphi_s \varphi_r.
\end{equation}

\section{Interferometric Light Scattering (iSCAT)}

The principle operation in the iSCAT methodology is the relative magnitude of mathematical terms in the optical field. The reference field is constant. The scattering field $R \approx k^4\alpha^2 = \frac{V^2}{\lambda^4}$. Similarly, the interference term scales as the square root of of the radius. Thus, the scattering term is dominated by the two terms, background and interference.

To accurately detect the particles if the scattering term is neglected, the contrast metric can be used. This metric relates the total field to that of a reference field;
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        C &= \frac{I - |E_r|^2}{|E_r|^2} \\
        &= \frac{|S|^2 + 2|R||S|\cos \Delta \varphi}{|R|^2}, \\
        &\xrightarrow{|S| \ll |R|} \frac{2|S|}{|R|}\cos\Delta \varphi.
    \end{aligned} 
\end{equation}

The phases in the interference field consists of a sum of optical fields. These fields acquire phase differences as they traverse through space. Aberration effects from optical transformations are also acquired as spatial and phase terms.

The total phase is dominated by the Guoy phase, which is the sum of accumulated phase as the light passes through a converging or diverging lens. The de-focusing phase is a relative phase shift due to misalignment in lens position which is described by the imaging condition equation.

Lastly, the phase difference from the inference term, which is compared to a Michelson-interferometer type system, is a dominant signal present directly in the iSCAT equation. 

The final image consists of the collected phase modulated optical fields and the aberrations from optical misalignment. These parameters must be considered when performing analysis on iSCAT images.

\section{3D Position Information from Optical Signal}

To obtain information from the iSCAT technique, the holographic images obtained by from the iSCAT metric must be related to the theoretical scattering pattern from Maxwell's relation. Specifically, this involves approximating the image captured on the camera to Eqn. \ref{eqn:rayleigh_scat}.

The interferometric point spread function is defined as the contrast metric of the scattering off of a sub-diffraction sized particle, 
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{iPSF} \equiv \frac{2E_r E_s \cos\phi_d}{E_r^2}.
\end{equation}

The Rayleigh scattering pattern has several parameters that have a degenerate effect on the iPSF \cite{dewitPreciseCharacterizationNanometerscale2023}. To avoid problems in the image to equation fitting, the parameterization developed in Ref. \cite{dewitPreciseCharacterizationNanometerscale2023} is used. This parameterization redefines the axial height and phase,
\begin{align}
    z'_p &= z_p - z_f\\
    \phi_0 &= -\phi_\mathrm{ref} + 2n_m k z_f + \arg(\alpha)\\
    E_0 &= \frac{E_i 4\sqrt{2}\pi^3|\alpha|}{E_r (\lambda/n_m)^3}.
\end{align}

This re-parameterization has been shown to have good agreement to complex models as demonstrated in Ref. \cite{dewitPreciseCharacterizationNanometerscale2023}. The final model is presented as the following equation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{iPSF} = 2 E_0 \frac{1}{n_m k r_p}\sqrt{1 + \left(z'_p/r_p\right)^2} \cos \left[-\left(\phi_0 + n_m k z'_p + n_m k r_p\right)\right].
\end{equation}

This function allows one to obtain the $z$ position of a particle with respect to the objective from image data.

\section{Resolution Limits of Optical Microscopy}

There is a limit of resolution that can be achieved through optical methods. The limit is described by the Abbe diffraction limit which states that the minimum distance between two resolvable particle is fixed by the light wavelength and objective's numerical aperture. 

Optical fields consists of a sum of plane waves that construct and deconstruct. The electric field can be decomposed into a Fourier series of exponential harmonics. 

To understand how the optical field modulates as it travels, the propagation equation can be written in a plane-wave basis,
\begin{equation}
	\vec E(x,y,z) = \iint_{\Omega} \vec E_0(k_x, k_y) e^{-i\left(x k_x + y k_y + \sqrt{k^2 - k_x^2 - k_y^2} \right)}dk_x dk_y, \quad \Omega = \{x \in \R : x^2 \leq k^2\}.
\end{equation}
The energy of the wave is fixed by the trigonometric relation of the transverse wave vectors, fixed by the relation $|k| = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$.

The propagated wave passes through an objective which can be modeled by a lens. Using the standard ABCD matrix formulation, the imaging condition must be satisfied,
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{z_1} + \frac{1}{z_2} = \frac{1}{f},
\end{equation}
where $z_1, z_2$ are the positions of the observer and the object, and $f$ is the focal distance of the lens. The magnification of the lens is defined as $M = z_2/z_1$.
 
To define a field formulation for this system, the propagation equation can be used. The incoming light that passes through the finite area of a lens is defined a pupil function $P(x,y)$ which is unitary if $r\leq R$, where $R$ is the radius of lens. The point spread function of the object is then defined as a delta-function through a two lens system. The measured optical field is given by the Fourier transform of the product of the pupil function and the delta-function.
\begin{eqnarray}
	H(x,y) = \iint_\Omega P(x',y')e^{\frac{ik}{2}\left(\frac{1}{z_1} - \frac{1}{f_1} - \frac{1}{f_2} + \frac{1}{z_2}\right)\sqrt{{x'}^2} + {y'}^2}e^{-\frac{ik}{z_2}(xx' + yy')}dx'dy'.
\end{eqnarray}
Expanding this field, one can use a zero-order Hankel transform to integrate over the radius and find that the point-spread function, $H$, is defined in terms of the numerical aperture and the magnification;
\begin{equation}
	H(r, z) = \int_0^1e^{-\frac{ik}{2}\frac{\text{NA}^2}{n}\rho^2 z}J_0\left(\frac{k\text{NA}}{M}\rho r\right) \rho d\rho,
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the rescaled radial vector $\rho= r'/R$, $\text{NA} = nR/f_1$, and $M = nf_2/f_1$.

In perfect focus, $z=0$, and the image is the point spread function. For $z\neq 0$ there is a defocusing aberration which is defined as the secondary exponential. For a point source, which is diffraction limited, the following is the limit of the optical microscope,
\begin{equation}
	H(r,0) = \left|\frac{J_1(k\text{NA}r)}{k\text{NA} r}\right|^2 .
\end{equation}
Solving for the minimum distance between the peak of the PSF and the minimum, the realtion $k\text{NA}r = x_0$ defines the minimum transverse imaging condition, where $x_0$ is the first root of $J_1(x)$. This is known as the transverse Abbe diffraction limit, 
\begin{equation}
	r_{\text{min}} = 0.61 \frac{\lambda}{\text{NA}}.
\end{equation}
Similar results are found for the minimum axial imaging criterion by setting $r=0$ and integrating. 
\begin{equation}
	z_{\text{min}} = \frac{2\lambda n}{\text{NA}^2}
\end{equation}

In the case of scattering microscopy, this is not a large problem as the scattering pattern is much larger, however, in the limit of two particle near each other, the spatial resolution limit must be enforced. 

 \section{Sources of Noise in Optical Imaging}

In imaging, there is a number of sources of aberrations that one must account for. The sources of noise depend on the optical system used. For a general imaging system, one only requires a single lens for imaging. This simple setup is shown in Fig \ref{fig:imaging_diagram_simple}.

    \centering

    \end{subfigure}
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.45\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/imaging_diagram.pdf}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{Simplest possible imaging setup. Image consists of a plane wave illumination, a lens, and a camera.}
    \label{fig:imaging_diagram_simple}
\end{figure}

For such a system, the sources of noise are the point spread function noise, Guoy phase aberrations, background noise, electronic noise, and spherical aberrations. 

The optical point spread noise originates from the transformations of light passing through optical components.

Each lens used in an optical path will collect light from the object plane, $U_o$ to a transformed plane, $U_i$. The propagation of that wave will collect aberration from the len's point spread function, as well as phase accumulation. The traversal over the point spread function is given by the equation,  
\begin{equation}
    U_i(x_i, y_i) = \iint_{\R^2} h(x_i, y_i, x_o, y_o) U_o(x_o, y_o) dx_o dy_o,
\end{equation} 
where $h(x_i, y_i, x_o, y_o)$ is known as the optical transfer function, or the response of a lens to a point-source. The len's magnification is incorporated into this using the following approximation;
\begin{equation}
    h(x_i, y_i, x_o, y_o) \approx K \delta(x_i \pm M x_o, y_i \pm M y_o)
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a complex constant and $M$ is a real-valued magnification. Each lens causes a small amount of aberration and misalignment which spatially limits the final optical field.

The phase accumulation, known as the Guoy phase, originates from plane waves converging to a gaussian beams when passed through a lens. The plane wave is tightened and forms a beam-waist around the len's focal point. As this focusing occurs, there is a phase that is accumulated and  equal to
\begin{equation}
    \psi(z) = \arctan\left(\frac{z}{z_R}\right), \quad z_r = \frac{\pi w_0^2 n}{\lambda},
\end{equation}
where $w_0$ is the waist of the final beam, $n$ is the refractive index, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light. In imaging, as long as the refractive index is constant, the phase is shift is equal to $\pi$. 

In optical microscopy where a refernence or excitation field is used, a background beam or illumination is present. The background noise profile will take the shape of the illumination profile alongside the intensity of the power density. 

The camera noise consists of electronic noise associated with the model and electronic design. For general CMOS cameras, the noise present will consist of photon noise from the detectors and readout noise from the data-transfer. These noises can be modeled as Poisson noise from the photon detections and a gaussian for the readout noise. 

In the case of using an imperfect lens, or a set of lenses as one has with an objective as one does in high magnification microscopy, there is a risk of spherical aberration. Spherical aberrations are caused by imperfections and misalignments in the lens itself. This cases the focal point of the lens to be slightly different depending on which area the light is focused by lens. For imaging purposes, spherical aberrations cause various parts of the image to be out of focus, while others are in focus, creating non-uniform images.
