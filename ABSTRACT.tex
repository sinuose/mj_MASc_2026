\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
The understanding of characteristics of the thermal movement of particles within a confined space has been studied extensively in order to understand how biological processes, such as actin-network dynamics \cite{fujiwara_confined_2016}, or chemical processes, such as design of $\mathrm{CO}_2$-capture devices \cite{gu_design_2026}. The sizing of nanoparticles, which are particulates with diameters in the range of $20\mathrm{ nm}$ to $200\mathrm{ nm}$, is a common measurement often intrinsically performed under confinement. However, despite the dynamics being theoretically established as a stochastic process, the role of electrostatic and hydrodynamic contributions remain an active field of interest.

% This is due to the fact that 3D particle-tracking is not used as often as other, higher throughput sizing techniques.
 By employing common techniques of nanoparticle tracking in tandem with a full theoretical model, the non-trivial effects of confinement on size-diffusivity were directly investigated and characterized. The work described in this thesis advances the method and tools of 3D nanoparticle analysis both theoretically and in application.

% This thesis provides an understanding and investigation into confined diffusion measurements.

The first section of the thesis describes a theoretical model of Brownian motion based on the confined-Langevin model using a Debye-Hückel electrostatic potential. The model was implemented, based on literature \cite{millanNumericalSimulationsConfined2023}, into a computer-based \textsc{Python} simulation in order to parameterize and create probability distributions of predicted particle motion. 

The second section describes a modified method of 3D tracking to obtain faster calculations of particle position from optical microscopy images. This was based on an algorithm described in Ref. \cite{dewitPreciseCharacterizationNanometerscale2023}. The validation of the method was performed on analytical simulation data using a model in Ref. \cite{Dastjerdi2022}. The model was evaluated in range of 1 nm of the ground truth.  

The third section describes the acquisition of  experimental data using a custom interferometric scattering microscope (iSCAT) with image pixel resolution 55 nm acquiring images between 400 and 1000 frames per second. The data consisted of images of scattering fringes from nanoparticle species and was analyzed using the algorithm developed in Chap. \ref{chap:ZEstimation}. The resultant information was accurate 3D particle tracks. The models developed in Chap. \ref{chap:brownianmotion} were applied to the tracks to obtain infer particle size and charge as shown in Chap. \ref{chap:expresult}.

\section*{Lay Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Lay Summary}

%MARTIN GOOD
Physical objects are understood to be comprised of smaller subunits. A majority of physically important molecules, such as plastic subunits, medicines, and food particles, are known as nanoparticles, which have diameters ranging from  $\SI{20}{\nm}$ to $\SI{200}{\nm}$. To ensure nanoparticles that are used in products are correctly produced, it is important to be able to measure their functional properties, and their size.

This thesis develops a method to measure the 3D position of nanoparticles using the scattering of light and applies the method to learn about how nanoparticles interact in confined volumes. 

This method has potential applications in nanoparticle production. The particle-surface interactions can be directly measured and quantified. Applications include lipid-nanoparticle based medicines, such as the COVID-19 mRNA-Vaccine \cite{hou_lipid_2021}, genetic targeted Onpattro vaccine \cite{akinc_onpattro_2019}, and most recently, novel cancer targeted mRNA-based immunotherapies vaccines. \cite{noauthor_personalized_2025}
