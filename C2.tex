\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{chap:expresult}

To validate the iSCAT imaging technique and computational methods described in previous sections, three major groups of experiments were conducted. One group focused on performing validation of confined diffusion of electrostatic motion of polystyrene beads. The second was investigating the transient mean-squared displacement behavior. The third one was done using lipid-nanoparticles in a PEG-passivated environment to look at diffusion in biological conditions. 

%, as discussed in Chap. \ref{chap:brownianmotion} and Chap. \ref{chap:langevinsimulations}.
The effects of electrostatic interaction of nanoparticle diffusion were investigated in various ionic concentrations. The salt concentration regulates the particle's effective confinement length. The change in confinement length directly affects how size-inferencing is performed. %The effects of changing the ionic concentration on the size inference from transverse and axial measurements were compared. 

The transient behavior and confined mean-squared displacement relations were performed on polystyrene bead diffusion by adding a glycerol solution to slow down the step-length of the particle's motion. Glycerol was chosen as the viscous reagent as the water-glycerol relation is well defined \cite{cheng_formula_2008} and has a lipid-membrane permeability \cite{Orbach1980, Walter1986}. 

To apply the theoretical models and statistical tools to a current, applicable biological system, the diffusion of lipid-nanoparticles was investigated within glass flow-cells. The effects of flow-cell poly-ethylene glycol passivation were measured. LNP size measurement techniques were compared and contrasted. LNP transient behavior was measured using glycerol. The complete list of samples used, as well as number of video and trajectories obtained from the data, is listed in Tab. \ref{tab:sample_list}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:methods}

The experiments described in this chapter were conducted using the wide-field iSCAT optical system as described in Chap. \ref{chap:iscatsetup}. The samples were placed within a cover-glass to create a flow-cell. The confinement system, convex-lens induced confinement, is presented below.

\subsection{Optical System Schematic}

The schematic of the optical system used in the laboratory experimentation is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:re_schematic}. To illuminate the samples, an illumination laser with wavelength of $\SI{405}{\nm}$ was used. This wavelength laser was selected to have the lowest cost and provide illumination within the visible light spectrum. The optical power of the laser was $\SI{240}{\milli \watt}$. The optical filter drops after the spatial filter to $\SI{120}{\milli \watt}$ which is $\sim 50\%$ of the initial laser output. 

The microscope objective was oil based with a  $100 \times$ optical magnification\footnote{(Nikon, Apochromat TIRF $100 \times$ oil immersion objective N.A. 1.49, W.D. $\SI{0.12}{\mm}$, F.O.V $\SI{22}{\mm}$)}. The images were captured by a sCMOS camera\footnote{(\textsc{pco.edge 4.2})}. The system was controlled by a custom computer software written by the Grant lab in the LabView programming language \cite{Brouwer2026}. The complete parts list of the optical system was presented in Tab. \ref{tab:components}.

\subsection{Glass Flow-cell Preparation}

The sample flow-cell device consisted of two glass borosilicate substrates \footnote{(Ted-Pella, product no.260372, $145 \pm \SI{15}{\micro \meter}$ thick, $25 \times \SI{25}{\mm}$ cover glass)}, separated by a $\SI{30}{\micro\meter}$ thick, double-sided adhesive spacer \footnote{(Nitto-Denko, product no. 5603)} as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:cg_photo}. The bottom coverslip has no etched features, while the top coverslip contains two $\SI{1}{mm}$ holes at opposite corners for fluidic injection through the chip.

    %\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/clic_chuck_very_good.pdf}

    \label{fig:cg_photo}
\end{figure}

The flow-cell is held in place by a metal plate holder and an acrylic chuck. The chuck had a printed shape which contained two fluidic inlets, screw holes that mounted overtop the metal chuck, and a central hole for the viewing. The metal chuck has a groove for the coverslip, as well as tapped holes for the mounted plastic chuck. The flow-cell is assembled using two $\mathrm{M}5$ screws. The assembled system is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:dia_photo}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/clic_chuck_very_good.pdf}
    \caption{flow-cell schematic and assembled flow-cell photograph.}
    \label{fig:dia_photo}
\end{figure}

To prepare the glass coverslips, the cover-glass was first cleaned of organic matter with piranha solution. The solution consisted of a $7:3$ sulfuric acid \footnote{(H2SO4, CAS: 7664-93-9, Thermo Fisher Scientific)}, hydrogen peroxide \footnote{(H2O2, CAS: 7722-84-1, Thermo Fisher Scientific)} mixture. Prior to piranha solution exposure, the cover-glass was exposed to toluene to rinse off excess organic compounds. An isopropyl alcohol soak was used to remove excess toluene film. Alternatively, an acetone soak followed by a Hellmanex detergent could be used in the place of toluene. Before exposure to piranha, the glass was washed with de-ionized water three consecutive times, or until the solution was without bubbles from the detergent. The cover-glass was soaked in the piranha for 1 hour. For experiments where PEG passivation was not performed, the cover-glass was assembled within 12 hours of piranha cleaning and using in imaging.

The passivation technique used for lipid-nanoparticle measurements was the 5 kDa PEG passivation as per \cite{kamanzi_single-particle_2026}. After the piranha cleaning, the cover-glass was etched in 1M KOH for 30 min at room temperature. The cover-glass was placed into a desiccator together with an APTES\footnote{((3-aminopropyl)-triethoxysilane, CAS: 919-30-2, ThermoFisher Scientific)} solution. The vacuum desiccator was set to 1.5 Torr for 30 min at room temperature. The cover-glass was removed and placed on a hotplate set to $\SI{120}{\degreeCelsius}$. The cover-glass was then exposed to succinimidyl-valerate (SVA) functionalized methoxy-PEG-valeric acid \footnote{(5K mPEGSVA, Laysan Bio)} in a 0.9 M sodium sulfate \footnote{(Na2SO4, CAS: 7757-82-6, Thermo Fisher Scientific)} solution for 30 min. Before imaging, the coverslips were thoroughly rinsed with deionized (DI) water  and dried with high-purity nitrogen gas. Pegylated cover-glass was assembled and stored at $\SI{-80}{\degreeCelsius}$ for up to 2 months. 

\subsection{Convex-lens Induced Confinement}

To confine the nanoparticles within the field of view of the objective, a convex-lens induced confinement, referred to as CLiC, was used. This method was developed by the Leslie Lab,  described in detail in the seminal publication referenced as per  \cite{berardPrecisionPlatformConvex2013}. 

A convex pusher lens is used together with a piezo linear actuator to displace the top coverslip of the flow-cell in a parabolic displacement. The piezo actuator is manually controlled during imaging and set to a specific, controlled height. The height is determined using the inference fringes of Newton's rings. The full method outline is visualized in Fig. \ref{fig:c7_fig1}


\begin{figure}[h!]

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/clic_very_good.pdf}
    \caption{Convex-lens induced confinement schematic. Two glass coverslips, separated by a $\SI{30}{\micro \meter}$ spacer are confined with a convex-lens pusher. The height between the coverslips is voltage-regulated to nanometer precision. Nanoparticle of radius $\SI{50}{\nm}$ pictured for reference.}

The experimental data consisted of videos of LNPs diffusing in a $\SI{220}{\nm}$ slit. The particle positions were measured using the iSCAT optical system and the trajectories were calculated from the video data. The spatial probability density histogram is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:lnp_pos_hist}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width]{Python/Figures/Chapter7/lnp_pos_hist.pdf}
    \caption{Probability histogram of LNP axial position. Imaged are onpattro standard, and bleb-like LNPs. Liposomal LNPs were too small to be tracked over a long-range.}
    \label{fig:lnp_pos_hist}
\end{figure}

The liposomal LNPs were not consistently detectable to allow for 2D tracking. The low detectability of the data may be related to the smaller size and index of refraction being closely related to that of the solution. The oil-core and bleb-like LNPs had enough detectability to calculate trajectory information. The probability density of the oil-core resembles a flat-top distribution indicating motion across the whole channel. The bleb-like lNPs appear to have a flat-top with a secondary central peak in the distribution indicating motion across the channel with a potential secondary interaction. 

To characterize the transient behavior, the LNPs were suspended in a 75\% glycerol solution and the video from the optical system was obtained. The three samples were imaged and their ensemble MSD was measured. This is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:lnp_gly_msd}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Python/Figures/LNP_glycerol_MSD.pdf}
    \caption{Ensemble MSD of LNP. Imaged are liposomal, onpattro standard, and bleb-like LNPs.}
    \label{fig:lnp_gly_msd}
\end{figure}

The left plot depicts the transverse MSD of the oil-core and bleb-like LNPs. The dotted blue line shows the oil-core LNP transverse diffusion. The solid light blue indicates the standard deviation. The dotted green line shows the bleb-like LNP transverse diffusion. The solid light green indicates the standard deviation. The right plot depicts the axial MSD of the oil-core and bleb-like LNPs. The dotted blue line shows the oil-core LNP axial diffusion. The solid light blue indicates the standard deviation. The dotted green line shows the bleb-like LNP axial diffusion. The solid light green indicates the standard deviation. The dotted red lines show the theoretical plateau values for particles of radius, $a$, in a $\SI{220}{\nm}$ slit. The transverse MSD is acceptable as it has a linear form near the first 20 lag-times. THe axial MSD, however, contains non-linear behavior and sporadic jumps. This noise indicates non-accurate axial tracking due to poor image data.
