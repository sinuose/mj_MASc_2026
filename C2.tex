\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{chap:expresult}

Using the iSCAT imaging technique and algorithms developed in this thesis, three different investigations were conducted. The effects of electrostatic interaction of nanoparticle diffusion was investigated. The ionic concentration, as discussed in Chap. [5], regulates the particles effective confinement length. The change in confinement length directly effects how size-inferencing is performed. The effects of changing ionic concentration of the size inference from transverse and axial measurements were compared. 

The transient behavior and confined mean-squared displacement relations were performed on polystyrene beads. To validate the transient behavior, glycerol was used to effectively slow down the step-length of the particle's motion. Glycerol as the viscosity relation is well defined \cite{cheng_formula_2008}. 

To apply the theoretical models and statistical tools to a current, applicable biological system, the diffusion of lipid-nanoparticles was conducted within glass flow-cells. The effects of flow-cell poly-ethylene glycol passivation were measured. Size-measurement techniques were compared and contrasted. Transient behavior was measured using glycerol. \subsection{Apparatus schematic}

The schematic of the optical system is shown in Fig \ref{fig:schematic} to remind the reader of the system. The optical system used for imaging is a wide-field iSCAT microscope. 

The optical system consisted of a $\SI{405}{\nm}$ excitation laser. The output of the laser was $\SI{240}{\milli \watt}$ before arriving at a spatial filter. The output after the spatial filter was $\SI{120}{\milli \watt}$. The imaging objective used was the $100 \times$ oil imaging objective (Nikon, Apochromat TIRF $100 \times$ oil immersion objective N.A. 1.49, W.D. $\SI{0.12}{\mm}$, F.O.V $\SI{22}{\mm}$). The camera used was the (\textsc{pco.edge 4.2}) sCMOS camera. Operating software was custom written by a lab-member of the Grant lab in the LabView programming language \red{REF}. 

\begin{figure}[t]
	\centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/schematic.pdf}
    \caption{ iSCAT Schematic. A 405 nm laser is sent through a spatial filter, passes through an aperture. The beam is sent through a focusing lens which is reflected off a polarizing beam splitter, polarized through a QWP focused on the back plane of the objective. \subsection{Glass Flow-cell Preparation}

The flow-cell device consisted of two glass substrates (Ted-Pella, product no.260372, $145 \pm \SI{15}{\micro \meter}$ thick, $25 \times \SI{25}{\mm}$ cover glass), separated by a 30 um thick double-sided adhesive spacer (Nitto-Denko, product no. 5603) as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:dia_photo}. The bottom coverslip is blank with no features. The top coverslip contains two $\SI{1}{mm}$ holes at two of the corners for fluidic injection.

The flow-cell is held in place by a metal plate holder and an acrylic chuck. The chuck contains a printed shape which contains fluidic inlets, as well as screw holes that mount overtop the metal chuck. The metal chuck has a groove for the coverslip, as well as tapped holes for the mounted plastic chuck. The flow-cell is assembled using two $\mathrm{M}3$ screws. The assembled system is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:dia_photo}.

\begin{figure}
    \caption{flow-cell schematic and assembled flow-cell photograph.}
    \label{fig:dia_photo}
\end{figure}

Before experimental use, The cover-glass is first cleaned with piranha solution. The solution consisted of a 7:3 sulfuric acid (H2SO4, CAS: 7664-93-9, Thermo Fisher Scientific), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, CAS: 7722-84-1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) mixture. prior to piranha exposure, the cover-glass was exposed to toluene to rinse off organic compounds. An isopropyl alcohol soak was used to remove excess toluene film. Alternatively, an acetone soak followed by a Hellmanex detergent soak was used in the place of toluene. Before exposure to piranha, the glass was washed with de-ionized water $3\times$, or until the solution was without bubbles from the detergent. The coverglass was soaked in the piranha for 1 hour. For experiments where PEG passivation was not performed, the coverglass was assembled within 12 hours of piranha cleaning and using in imaging.

The passivation technique used in this thesis was the 5 kDa PEG passivation used in \cite{kamanzi_single-particle_2026}. Cover-glass is first piranha cleaned for 1 hour. 
The cover-glass was etched in 1 M KOH for 30 min at room temperature. The cover-glass was placed into a desicator together with an APTES ((3-aminopropyl)-triethoxysilane, CAS: 919-30-2, ThermoFisher Scientific) solution. The vacuum desiccator was set to 1.5 Torr for 30 min at room temperature. The cover-glass was removed and placed on a hotplate set to $\SI{120}{\degreeCelsius}$. The cover-glass was then exposed to succinimidyl-valerate (SVA) functionalized methoxy-PEG-valeric acid (5K mPEGSVA, Laysan Bio) in a 0.9 M sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, CAS: 7757-82-6, Thermo Fisher Scientific) solution for 30 min. Before imaging, the coverslips were thoroughly rinsed with deionized (DI) water  and dried with high-purity nitrogen gas. Pegylated cover-glass was assembled and stored at $\SI{-80}{\degreeCelsius}$ for up to 2 months. 

\subsection{Convex-lens induced confinement}

The CLiC system was used to achieve nanometer confinement while imaging. This method is described in detail in the seminal publication \cite{berardPrecisionPlatformConvex2013}. A convex pusher lens is used together with a piezo linear actuator to displace the top coverslip of the flow-cell in a parabolic displacement. The piezo actuator is manually controlled during imaging and set to a specific, controlled height. The height is determined using the inference fringes of Newton's rings. \section{Results of iSCAT experiments}

% mission statement
A series of experiments were conducted to use the mathmatical method presented in the previous section to find the nanoparticle size and compare it to a known value of $100$ nm carboxylate-modified polystyrene beads. Effects of electrostatic interactions were investigated using piranha-cleaned borosilicate glass coverslips and phosphate-buffered saline solution (PBS). The diffusivity profile of transient motion was investgated using glycerol-water solution to modify the viscosity of the media.  A solution of 75\% glycerol with 25\% 1x PBS was used to measure transient-diffusion effects of $\SI{100}{\nm}$ carboxylate-modified polystyrene beads at a frame rate of $\SI{1000}{\fps}$. The effects of confinement on lipid nanoparticles, three formulations of lipid nanoparticle were imaged using iSCAT. To prevent sticking to the glass coverslips, a two-step poly-ethelyene glycol passivation technique was used. 

\section{Electrostatics Effects on Polystyrene Bead Diffusion in PBS}
\chaptermark{Elec. Eff. on PS. Bead Diff. in PBS}

The effect of ionic concentration on 100 nm polystyrene beads were performed. The beads were suspended in a series of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solutions with varying ionic concentrations. Ten videos were obtained according to the method described in \ref{sec:methods}. The videos are large ($\sim \SI{30}{\gb}$ per video) and are contained in a zip file upon request. 

For each measurement, $\SI{100}{\nm}$ diameter beads were confined into a $\SI{220}{\nm}$ slit verified using inference fringes. The output from each video was a collection of trajectories. For each concentration, the trajectories were reconstructed in three-dimensional space. The number of particles for each concentration of PBS are listed; $0.1\%, 8$, $0.01\%, 53$, $0.001\%, 13$, $0.00001\%, 62$. To visualize the trajectory density for different ionic concentrations, the trajectories of the diffusion are combined into density plots. 

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Python/Figures/density.pdf}
    \caption{Spatial density of the average trajectory of particles. }
    \label{fig:trajectory_ensemble}
\end{figure}


The resulting videos allowed to measure the three-dimensional particle position in a confined height of $\SI{220}{\nm}$. The positional probability density of particle motion is  in Fig. \ref{fig:c7_fig3}. 

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Python/Figures/Chapter7/c7_fig3.pdf}
    \caption{Probability density for three different concentrations of PBS. Blue has a concentration of $0.1\%$ PBS, green has a concentration of $0.01\%$ PBS, and orange has a concentration of $0.00001\%$ PBS. The horizontal axis is the axial ($z$-axis) position of the particle. The vertical axis is the frequency of position measurement.}
    \label{fig:c7_fig3}
\end{figure}

Trace blue and green have the same distribution. The difference between peaks is normalization artifact. Trace violet does not following the same distribution, but rather is flat. The diffusion in blue and green is screened, where the particle does not explore the full height. In violet the particle does explore the well more. As the concentration of PBS increases, the distribution becomes more uniform, while in the higher concentrations, the particle is focused between the two plates. This experiment agrees with the simulations done as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:brown_realization}. The individual $\mathrm{FWHM}$ of this distribution is plotted compared to the PBS concentration. This is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:FWHM_PBS}.

\section{Polystyrene Transient Diffusion}

To investigate if transient diffusion information can be obtained with this method, polystyrene beads were once again used. The beads were suspended in 45\% glycerol and 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solutions. Ten videos, of length 10000 frames each, were obtained according to the method described in \ref{sec:methods}. The videos are large ($\sim \SI{30}{\gb}$ per video) and are contained in a zip file upon request. 

The glycerol was adjusted to ensure that the diffusion coefficient was around $10\times$ smaller so that the mean step length was $\sim \SI{10}{\nm}$. This was done using the glycerol-water weight curve. This curve is shown in  Fig. \ref{fig:gly_curve}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Python/Figures/glycerol_curve.pdf}
    \caption{glycerol-water curve. The curve is taken from \cite{cheng_formula_2008}.}
    \label{fig:gly_curve}
