% In much of nature, the idea of size scales is widely discussed and investigated. From a basic understanding of physics, one understands that the forces an object feels are directly linked to their mass or volume and mass density. For example, we can easily understand what will happen to a basketball if we throw it up into the air as we are sitting on planet earth. However, when we leave the familar size scale, such as having a basketball the size of planet earth, or the size of a gas molecule, our intuition begins to show its cracks. 

Objects in nature fall into two size-categories: the macroscopic and the microscopic. Macroscopic
objects can be directly measured with a ruler or a photograph as they are much larger than compared to light. However, as the size drops below the wavelength of visible light, they become un-resolvable by the light alone. The diffraction limits the resolvable size to roughly half of the wavelength that a microscope can observe. 
Recovering the dimensions of anything smaller requires indirect measurements.

This limitation is of utmost significance; in biological systems, size is not only a geometric attribute, but is proportional to the functional properties. Consider, mRNA-lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNP), whose therapeutic effect depends on how efficiently the mRNA is released into the cellular environment. This release depends on endosomal escape, a strongly size-selective process \cite{Sahay2013, Gilleron2013, Rejman2004, Zhang2009, Banerjee2016}. Since the potency varies with a length-scale that is below the size-limit of an optical microscope, the precise measurement  of particle size is essential to reliable pharmacological production and quality control. 

In the limit of microscopic objects smaller than the wavelength of light, such as particles $\sim \SI{150}{\nm}$, the size becomes un-resolvable by the light alone \cite{Steck2021, Born1999}. In order to measure the size, one must use alternative approaches.

One universal property of sub-micron particles is that they undergo an apparent size-related stochastic motion when suspended in media \cite{brownXXVIIBriefAccount1828, einstein1956investigations, bian111YearsBrownian2016}. At the nano-scale, thermal energy, $k_bT$, is comparable to, or larger than, the gravitational and inertial energy scales that typical govern macroscopic dynamics. In the case of continuous media made of atomic subunits, the thermal energy emerges into a motion when the media's Reynold's numbers is much lower than one \cite{bian111YearsBrownian2016}, \[\Re \ll 1.\] In this environment, the media's viscous drag dominates over the inertial force, which constitutes a memoryless, random motion \footnote{This is also a function of temporal resolution \cite{bian111YearsBrownian2016}.}. 

The relation between the particle's size and its motion is explained in Einstein's seminal paper \cite{einstein1956investigations} where the rate of transport, or diffusion, is related to the particle's hydrodynamic radius,
\begin{equation}
    D(\vec{x}, a) = \frac{k_bT}{\gamma(\vec{x}, a)},
\end{equation}

where $\gamma(\vec{x}, a)$ is the viscous drag at the position $\vec{x}$ of a particle of hydrodynamic radius $a$. In the case of a spherical paricle in an incompressible fluid, $\gamma(a) = 6\pi \eta a$, where $\eta$ is the fluid viscosity. 

In order for the size estimate obtained from stochastic particle trajecories to be statistically relevant, the number of data points in the positional trajecory must be sufficient. Since the statistical uncertainty of the diffusion coefficient scales as $\sim\sqrt{N}$, a higher number of particle locations yield a more precise size inference \cite{vestergaardOptimalEstimationDiffusion2014}. 

Long particle trajectories are typically obtained using imaging the particle position over time using optical microscopy. In optical measurements, the long-particle trajectory requirement favors the use of a scattering microscopy over fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescent microscopy utilizes excited fluorophores to produce signal. These fluorophores have finite lifetimes, to which they oxidize and stop producing light, known as photobleaching \cite{taylor_interferometric_2018}. In addition, fluorescent microscopes, although possible to obtain three-dimensional position, are often only accurate for two-dimensional localization \textcolor{red}{REF}. 

In order to obtain large number of particle positions, while maintaining independent particle specificity, particles are often spatially confined within the field of view of an optical imaging system for an extended period of time \red{REF?}. Without this axial confinements, particles rapidly diffuse out of the focal plane. In prior work, convex-lens induced confinement (CLiC) measurements have typically utilized micro-apertures, $\sim \SI{1.5}{\micro \meter^3}$, in order to confine single particles for temporally extended measurements via fluorescence microscopy \cite{kamanziSimultaneousSingleParticleMeasurements2021,kamanzi_single-particle_2026,graves_direct_2026}. In complement, Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) measurements follow the same principles, where flow is applied to particles confined between two plates and are imaged using side-scattering microscopy \cite{filipeCriticalEvaluationNanoparticle2010}. in NTA, however, particles are still able to diffuse in and out of the focal volume.

Although experimentally feasible, confinement intrisicaly modifies the size-diffusivity relation rendering it non-trivial by introducing a position dependent viscous drag. This non-trivial behavior can been shown to alter the diffusivity of a particle by a significant amount \cite{swanParticleMotionParallel2010,bian111YearsBrownian2016}. 

Since the diffusivity of a particle in confinement is a function of position, in order to recover the true size of a particle diffusing, it is necessary to have the full spatial trajectory of the particle's motion with respect to the confinement barriers. As such, two dimensional tracking alone is insufficient; without the axial dimensions the spatially dependent correction cannot be inferred. Inverting the size-diffusivity relation demands the full three-dimensional trajectory, in particular the axial distance to the wall. 

In order to obtain an optical trace of three-dimensional scattering, interference methods, such as interferometric light-scattering microscopy, are highly favored as typical fluroescence measurements lack an extended temporal measurement of three-dimensional position. In addition, many commonly used  optical methods are limited to only two-dimensional slices of a focal plane, which is typically on the order of a few hundred nanometers.  

Thus, this thesis establishes a three-dimensional tracking method of confined diffusion and builds on applications of measurement of the size-diffusivity relationship, where the z-motion is used to evaluate the position-dependent confinement as well as providing complementary particle size information that is independent on the media viscosity. The thesis consists of 8 chapters. Chapter 2 provides a description of the literature of confined brownian motion over the last century. Chapter 3 describes the theoretical description of interferometric scattering microscopy (iSCAT) and physical limits that apply in optical theory. Chapter 4 discusses the application of Brownian motion and iSCAT in a biological context, which details regarding lipid-nanoparticles and why they need to be studied on the single-molecule level. Chapter 5 consists of investigations of confined diffusion using Langevin simulations. A simulated system was developed and the size-diffusivity relation in an electrostatically screened system was investigated. Chapter 6 describes the 3D iSCAT tracking technique developed during this work. The method overview and validation and explicitly developed and validated Chapter 7 describes the method applied to experimental data and utilizes various nanoparticle species, highlighting their varied diffusions. Chapter 8 concludes the world and provides a future outlook on the topic and steps which need to be advanced.
