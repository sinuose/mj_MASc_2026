Knowing an object's size is paramount to characterizing it's properties in nature. One such example is that of the lipid nanoparticle (LNP) based medicines. LNP-based drugs are dependent on a size-selective process in cells known as endosomal escape \cite{Sahay2013, Gilleron2013}. This process is highly sensitive to particle size, making it a very important characteristic to keep track of \cite{Rejman2004, Zhang2009, Banerjee2016}.  

In the limit of microscopic objects  smaller than the wavelength of light, the size become un-resolvable by the light alone \cite{Steck2021, Born1999}. In order to measure the size, one must use other approaches to obtain size.

One universal property that sub-micron particles undergo is an apparent size-related stochastic motion when suspended in media. At the nano-scale, thermal energy, $k_bT$, is comparable to, or larger than the gravitational and inertial energy scales that typical govern macroscopic dynamics. In the case of liquids, the thermal energy emerges into a motion in Reynold's numbers $\Re \ll 1$, where the viscous drag dominates ver the inertia. 

The relation between the particle's size and its motion is explained in Einstein's seminal paper \cite{einstein1956investigations} where the rate of transport, or diffusion is related to the particle's size,
\begin{equation}
    D(\vec{x}, a) = \frac{k_bT}{\gamma(\vec{x}, a)},
\end{equation}

where $\gamma(\vec{x}, a)$ is the viscous drag at the position $\vec{x}$ of a particle of hydrodynamic radius $a$. In the case of a spherical paricle in an incompressible fluid, $\gamma(a) = 6\pi \eta a$, where $\eta$ is the fluid viscosity. 

In order for size estimate obtained from stochastic particle trajecories to be statistically relevant the number of data points must be sufficiently long. Since the statistical uncertainty of the diffusion coefficient scales as $\sim\sqrt{N}$, higher number of particle locations yield a more precise size inference \cite{vestergaardOptimalEstimationDiffusion2014}. In optical measurements, this requirement favors the use of a scattering microscopy over fluorescence as fluorescent fluorophores have a finite lifetime before photobleaching, rendering localization of them inert \cite{taylor_interferometric_2018}. In addition, fluorescent microscopes, although possible to obtain z-information, have lower content than compared to scattering techniques \textcolor{red}{REF}. 

In order to obtain large number of particle positions, which maintaining particle specificity, particles are spatially confined within the field of view of an optical imaging system for an extended period of time. For instance, convex-lens induced confinement (CLiC) measurements typically use micro-apertures of volume $\SI{1.5}{\micro \meter^3}$ to confine single molecules for extended temporal measurements via fluorescence microscopy \cite{kamanziSimultaneousSingleParticleMeasurements2021,kamanzi_single-particle_2026,graves_direct_2026}. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) measurements follow the same principles where a flow is applied to particles confined between two plates in a flow-cell and are imaged using scattering microscopy \cite{filipeCriticalEvaluationNanoparticle2010}.

Although experimentally more feasible, confinement intrisicaly modifies the size-diffusivity relation, rendering it non-trivial by introducing a position dependent viscous drag. This non-trivial behavior can been shown to alter the diffusivity of a particle by a significant amount \cite{swanParticleMotionParallel2010,bian111YearsBrownian2016}. 

Since the diffusivity of a particle in confinement is a function of position, in order to recover the true size of a particle diffusing, it is necessary to have the full spatial trajectory of the particle's motion with respect to the confinement barriers. As such, two dimensional tracking alone is insufficient; without the axial dimensions the spatially dependent correction cannot be inferred.Inverting the size-diffusivity relation demands the full three-dimensional trajectory, in particular the axial distance to the wall. 

In order to obtain an optical trace of three-dimensional scattering, interference methods, such as interferometric light-scattering microscopy, are highly favored as typical fluroescence measurements lack an extended temporal measurement of three-dimensional position.In addition, many commonly used  optical methods are limited to only two-dimensional slices of a focal plane, which is typically on the order of a few hundred nanometers. Without this axial confinements, particles rapidly diffuse out of the focal plane. 

Thus, this thesis establishes a three-dimensional tracking method of confined diffusion and builds on applications of measurement of the size-diffusivity relationship.

Chapter 5 consists of investigations of confined diffusion using Langevin simulations.
