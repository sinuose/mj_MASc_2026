From a basic understanding of physics, one understands that the forces an object feels are directly linked to their mass or volume and mass density. For example, we can easily understand what will happen to a basketball if we throw it up into the air as we are sitting on planet earth. However, when we leave the familar size scale, such as having a basketball the size of planet earth, or the size of a gas molecule, our intuition begins to show its cracks. 

Objects that exist in nature can be divided into macroscopic and microscopic entities. Macroscopic objects can be directly measured with a ruler or a photograph. However, as microscopic objects become smaller than the wavelength of light, they become un-resolveable by the light alone. Thus, one must resort to other methods to recover their size. 

At the nanoscale, thermal energy, $k_bT$, is comparable to, or larger than the gravitational and inertial energy scales that typical govern macroscopic dynamics. In the case of liquids, the thermal energy emerges into a motion in Reynold's numbers $\Re \ll 1$, where the viscous drag dominates ver the inertia. The result is a stochastic, thermal motion that governs particle transport. 

The relation between the particle's size and its motion is explained in Einstein's seminal paper \cite{einstein1956investigations} where the rate of transport, or diffusion is related to the particle's size,
\begin{equation}
    D(\vec{x}, a) = \frac{k_bT}{\gamma(\vec{x}, a)},
\end{equation}

where $\gamma(\vec{x}, a)$ is the viscous drag at the position $\vec{x}$ of a particle of hydrodynamic radius $a$. In the case of a spherical paricle in an incompressible fluid, $\gamma(a) = 6\pi \eta a$, where $\eta$ is the fluid viscosity. 

In typical measurements of particle motion, it is most tractable when the particle is confined within the microscope's field of view for an extended period of time. This is best achieved using spatial confinement. For instance, convex-lens induced confinement (CLiC) measurements typically use micro-apertures of volume $\SI{1.5}{\micro \meter^3}$ to confine single molecules for extended temporal measurements via fluorescence microscopy \cite{kamanziSimultaneousSingleParticleMeasurements2021,kamanzi_single-particle_2026,graves_direct_2026}. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) measurements follow the same principles where a flow is applied to particles confined between two plates in a flow-cell and are imaged using scattering microscopy \cite{filipeCriticalEvaluationNanoparticle2010}. The confinement is performed as microscope images consist of 2D slices of a focal plane, which is typically on the order of a few hundred nanometers. Without this axial confinements, particles rapidly diffuse out of the focal plane. 

Although experimentally convienient, the viscous drag relation is a function of position. In turn, size-diffusivity relation in confinement is intrinsically non-trivial. This non-trivial behavior can been shown to alter the diffusivity of a particle by a significant amount \cite{swanParticleMotionParallel2010,bian111YearsBrownian2016}. 

Since the diffusivity of a particle in confinement is a function of position, in order to recover the true size of a particle diffusing, it is necessary to have the full spatial trajectory of the particle's motion with respect to the confinement barriers. Inverting the size-diffusivity relation demands the full three-dimensional trajectory, in particular the axial distance to the wall. As such, two dimensional tracking alone is insufficient; without the axial dimensions the spatially dependent correction cannot be inferred.

For the size estimate to be statistically relevant the trajectory must be sufficiently long. Since teh statistical uncertainty scales as $\sim\sqrt{N}$, more frames yield a more precise size inference \cite{vestergaardOptimalEstimationDiffusion2014}. This requirement favors the use of a scattering microscopy over fluorescence as fluorescent fluorophores have a finite lifetime before photobleaching, rendering localization of them inert \cite{taylor_interferometric_2018}.

In the realm of biological applications, there are many such examples where such a measurement is of interest. One such case is that of the lipid nanoparticle (LNP). Lipid nanoparticle formulation processes generate populations of heterogenous LNPs \cite{ma_mrna_2025}. Bulk measurement methods alone cannot resolve these heterogeneities, which single-molecule methods are required \cite{ma_mrna_2025}. However, without proper correction for confinement effects, these single molecule measurements will not be accurate.
