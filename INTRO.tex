Objects in nature fall into approximately two size-categories: the macroscopic and the microscopic. Macroscopic objects can be directly measured with a ruler or a photograph. However, as the size drops below the wavelength of visible light, they become un-resolvable by light alone. Atomic methods such as x-ray diffraction or neutron scattering, although able to obtain size and structure information, often require prepared or perturbed samples. Visible light, however, does not have this limitation and can observe particles in their natural, unperturbed state. Due to the Abbe diffraction limit, the smallest optical resolvable size of a particle is half of the wavelength of light \cite{Steck2021, Born1999}. Recovering the dimensions of anything smaller, or sub-diffraction, requires indirect measurements.

The Abbe diffraction limit plays a significant role in observing biological systems with sizes that are sub-diffraction. In biological systems, size of proteins, enzymes, and organelles are not only a geometric property, but they are also relate to the functional properties \cite{Hou2019MoleculesReview}. Thus, the size is a critical parameter for biological systems.

For mRNA-lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNP), whose therapeutic effect depends on how efficiently the mRNA is released into the cellular environment, size is utmost importance. The release of mRNA into the cytoplasm depends on a size-selective biological process known as endosomal escape \cite{Sahay2013, Gilleron2013, Rejman2004, Zhang2009, Banerjee2016}. Since the potency of the mRNA-LNP varies with a parameter related to the size of the particle, observing it in the biological environment gives incredible insight to the chemical process \cite{Chen2016}. Thus, the precise measurement of particle size is essential to reliable pharmacological production, quality control, and rational design.

One universal property of sub-micron particles is that they undergo an apparent size-related stochastic motion when suspended in media \cite{brownXXVIIBriefAccount1828, einstein1956investigations, bian111YearsBrownian2016}. At the nano-scale, thermal energy, $k_bT$, is the dominant force that acts on the particle. The gravitational and inertial energy, that typical govern macroscopic dynamics, are negligible in this length-scale. In liquid, or continuous media, the viscous drag dominates over the inertial force, which constitutes a memory-less, random motion \footnote{This is also a function of temporal resolution \cite{bian111YearsBrownian2016}.}. The emergence of this thermal motion occurs when the ratio of the inertial forces to viscous forces, known as the Reynold's number, is much lower than one \cite{bian111YearsBrownian2016}, \[\Re \ll 1.\]

The relation between the particle's size and its motion is explained in Einstein's seminal paper \cite{einstein1956investigations}. Einstein showed that the diffusion coefficient, and implicitly the size, can be extracted from the variance of particle displacement.

In order for the size estimate obtained from stochastic particle trajectories to be statistically relevant, the number of data points in the positional trajectory must be sufficient. Since the statistical uncertainty of the diffusion coefficient scales as $\sim\sqrt{N}$, a higher number of particle locations yield a more precise size inference \cite{vestergaardOptimalEstimationDiffusion2014}. 

Long particle trajectories are typically obtained by imaging the particle position over a period of time using optical microscopy. One common method of optical microscopy is to use fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescence microscopy utilizes covalently bonded fluorophore dyes to produce optical signal under laser excitation. Although fluorescence signal is high, the fluorophores have finite lifetimes, to which they oxidize and stop producing light, known as photobleaching \cite{taylor_interferometric_2018}. Alternatively, scattering microscopy is a technique that utilizes the scattering of light off of particle boundaries to obtain signal. This technique requires computational processing to realize signal. This process can be imaged indefinitely without loss in signal as particles are not damaged by the light alone.

% The requirement of having many observations favors the use of a scattering microscopy over fluorescence microscopy. In addition, fluorescent microscopes, although possible to obtain three-dimensional position, are often only accurate for two-dimensional localization \textcolor{red}{REF}. In addition, many commonly used optical methods are limited to only two-dimensional slices of a focal plane, which is typically on the order of a few hundred nanometers.  

In order to obtain large number of particle positions, while maintaining independent particle specificity, particles are often spatially confined within the field of view of an optical imaging system for an extended period of time \cite{Hou2019MoleculesReview}. Without axial confinement, particles rapidly diffuse out of the focal plane. In recent scientific publications, convex-lens induced confinement (CLiC) measurements have typically utilized micro-geometries of volume $\sim \SI{1.5}{\micro \meter^3}$ in order to confine single particles for temporally extended measurements via fluorescence microscopy \cite{kamanziSimultaneousSingleParticleMeasurements2021,kamanzi_single-particle_2026,graves_direct_2026}. In complement, Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) measurements follow the same principles, where flow is applied to particles confined between two plates and are imaged using side-scattering microscopy \cite{filipeCriticalEvaluationNanoparticle2010}. In NTA, however, particles are still able to diffuse in and out of the focal volume.

Although experimentally feasible, confinement in small spaces influences the motion of particles near surfaces. This property intrinsically modifies the size-diffusivity relation. This effect is non-trivial, introducing a position dependent viscous drag that has been shown to alter the diffusivity by a significant amount \cite{swanParticleMotionParallel2010,bian111YearsBrownian2016}. 

To account for near-wall effects, the particle must be tracked in three-dimensions with respect to the confinement barriers. Two dimensional tracking, which is commonly performed with optical microscopy alone, is theoretically insufficient to recover the true size and to find spatially varying diffusion coefficient \cite{Welsher2025Hydrogels}. Without tracking the axial dimensions, the spatially dependent correction is uncertain. 

To measure the nanoparticle properties, such as diffusivity and size, this thesis presents an advancement of the iSCAT technique allowing for rapid and accurate three-dimensional tracking reconstruction. From the three-dimensional trajectory, the diffusivity, size, and environment conditions can be easily determined. Additionally, the confined axial size-measurement technique is shown to be a media insensitive approach; complementary to trajectory-based methods.

Chapter 4 discusses the application of Brownian motion and iSCAT in a biological context, which details regarding lipid-nanoparticles and why they need to be studied on the single-molecule level. Chapter 5 consists of investigations of confined diffusion using Langevin simulations. A simulated system was developed and the size-diffusivity relation in an electrostatic-ally screened system was investigated. Chapter 6 describes the 3D iSCAT tracking technique developed during this work. The method overview and validation and explicitly developed and validated Chapter 7 describes the method applied to experimental data and utilizes various nanoparticle species, highlighting their varied diffusions.
